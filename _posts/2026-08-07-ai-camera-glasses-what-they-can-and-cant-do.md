We mentioned [AI smart glasses]({{ '/top-5-ai-robots-smart-gadgets-china/' | relative_url }}) in
our first roundup as a genuinely useful hands-free AI assistant. That's still true — but "AI
glasses" as a category covers a wide capability range, and listing photos tend to show the best
case, not the typical one. Here's a more grounded look at what to actually expect.

## What they're actually good at right now

**Hands-free photo and video capture.** This is the most reliable feature across almost every
model — a camera built into the frame that captures what you're looking at without pulling out a
phone. It works consistently because it doesn't depend on AI interpretation, just a button press or
voice command.

**Real-time translation of text and speech.** Pointing the glasses at a menu or sign and getting a
translated readout, or having spoken conversation translated through the built-in speakers, is a
mature enough feature at this point to be genuinely useful — it's essentially the same underlying
tech as the translation earbuds we've covered, just repackaged into a different form factor.

**Basic voice assistant access.** Asking a question and getting a spoken answer works about as
well as talking to a phone assistant, because that's largely what's happening — the glasses are a
microphone/speaker peripheral for a cloud AI model, not doing heavy processing locally.

## Where the gap between marketing and reality shows up

**Battery life is still the real constraint.** Packing a camera, speakers, microphones, and a
wireless connection into a normal-sized glasses frame doesn't leave much room for battery. Expect
real-world active use closer to a few hours than a full day — fine for a dinner out, not something
you wear all day recording continuously.

**Most models don't have a real display.** Despite "AR glasses" branding on some listings, most AI
camera glasses in this price range are audio-only — no heads-up display overlay. If a listing shows
a floating interface in the lens, check carefully whether that's an actual display or a marketing
render; genuine HUD/waveguide display glasses exist, but they're a different (and considerably more
expensive) product category than the camera-and-speaker glasses most listings are selling.

**"Understands what it sees" is doing a lot of work in ad copy.** Object and scene recognition
exists, but it's closer to "identifies broad categories reasonably well" than genuine scene
understanding — don't expect it to reliably read complex context, notice subtle details, or explain
*why* something is happening, just describe what's broadly present.

**Social and practical friction is real.** A camera pointed outward, worn on your face, reads
differently to other people than a phone camera does. Recording in public, in someone's home, or in
a meeting carries a different social weight than most buyers initially expect — worth thinking
through before it's a purchase decision made purely on features.

## So who should actually buy a pair?

If you want hands-free photo/video capture and real-time translation, current AI camera glasses
deliver on that reliably, and that alone may be worth it for travel or accessibility use cases. If
you're expecting a genuine heads-up AR display or deep contextual scene understanding — the kind of
"AI glasses" often depicted in concept marketing — that's a different, pricier product tier, and
worth researching the exact display specs before buying rather than assuming the glasses category
is one uniform thing.
